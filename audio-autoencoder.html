Generative models · Audio

Complex-valued autoencoder for machinery audio

A neural network that learns to rebuild CNC machine sound, so virtual factories and VR training can sound like the real machine.

Problem
Virtual factories and VR training show machines but rarely sound like them, and there is little machine audio to train AI on.
What I built
A neural network that learns to compress and rebuild machining sound, keeping both its loudness and phase.
Result
Rebuilt recorded machining sound almost exactly after 6 hours of training on 4 hours of audio.
Why it matters
A first step toward generating realistic machine sound for operator training and sound-based fault spotting. Peer-reviewed in Manufacturing Letters (2025).
Role
First author; model design, training, evaluation
When
2023 – 2025, ASU
Published
Manufacturing Letters 44 (2025), NAMRC 53

The problem

Virtual factories already use cameras, thermal sensors and vibration data, but almost none use sound, even though machinists and monitoring systems rely on it to catch tool wear and spindle problems early. Generating realistic machine audio for each process condition would let a virtual factory or VR trainer sound like the real shop floor. Unlike speech and music, though, there is very little machinery audio to train on, so the model has to learn from a small dataset.

Approach

I designed an autoencoder, a neural network that compresses data and then rebuilds it. It works on the sound’s short-time Fourier transform (STFT), a time–frequency picture of the audio made of complex numbers. Feeding the real and imaginary parts in as two channels lets the network learn loudness and phase together, so its output converts straight back to sound.

  1. Cut the recording into 0.74-second clips and compute each clip’s STFT.
  2. Compress and rebuild it with 2D convolution layers.
  3. Convert the rebuilt STFT back into audio.
  4. Compare real and rebuilt audio in time and frequency.

Training used my 4-hour CNC audio dataset on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 workstation.

Results

8 × 10⁻⁴error rebuilding recorded audio (mean squared error, 1,000 clips)
≈ 0.20error of newly generated audio (20 clips)
6 hoursto train; about 4–5 hours to retrain
0.74 → 1.48 sclip length doubled by a second decoder

The rebuilt audio reproduced most of the frequencies in the real recordings. A second decoder with skip connections doubled the clip length and lowered the generated-audio error to about 0.11 after 24 hours of training. The model needs little data, but it is not yet fast enough for real-time use. I presented the work at NAMRC 53 with an NSF travel award.
